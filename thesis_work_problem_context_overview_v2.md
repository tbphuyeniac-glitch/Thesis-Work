# Thesis Work Overview: Problem, Context, and Expected Output

## 1. Research Context

This thesis studies an **Inventory Routing Problem (IRP)** in a retail distribution setting with one **Central Distribution Center (DC / Central Warehouse)** and multiple retail stores. The practical motivation comes from a real-world retail environment in which the DC is responsible for replenishing stores, while stores experience different inventory conditions over time due to daily sales fluctuations.

In a conventional centralized distribution system, the DC creates a replenishment plan and ships products to stores according to expected demand. However, even when the initial plan is reasonable, actual demand at store level may differ from the forecast. As a result, some stores may face shortages while others still hold excess inventory. This mismatch motivates the use of **lateral transshipment (LT)**, meaning stock transfers between stores after the original DC-to-store replenishment plan has already been executed.

Therefore, the thesis focuses on a **two-stage operational logic**:

1. **Baseline replenishment stage**: the DC supplies stores based on planned or forecast demand.
2. **Adjustment stage under demand fluctuation**: when actual demand deviates from the baseline plan, stores may exchange inventory through lateral transshipment to reduce shortages and overall system cost.

This setting is more realistic than assuming that the initial replenishment plan is always perfectly accurate.

---

## 2. Problem Background

The baseline retail problem can be understood as follows:

- A single DC holds inventory and dispatches products to stores.
- Each store has product demand over multiple periods.
- The system must decide how much inventory to send from the DC to each store over time.
- Transportation, inventory holding, shortage, and operational feasibility must be considered.

If only the baseline DC-to-store replenishment process is modeled, then the solution assumes that planned demand is sufficiently accurate. In practice, however, store-level demand is uncertain or may change after the baseline plan is determined.

To reflect this operational reality, the thesis introduces a **demand disturbance mechanism**, referred to as a **shocking factor**. This shocking factor alters the original demand after the baseline plan is produced. In other words:

- The system first computes a baseline replenishment plan using expected demand.
- Then the realized demand is generated by applying a shocking factor to the baseline demand.
- Because of this change, some stores may become under-supplied and some over-supplied relative to actual realized demand.
- At that point, lateral transshipment becomes necessary as a recovery or adjustment mechanism.

So, lateral transshipment is **not the primary sourcing method**, but rather a **corrective recourse action** after demand deviations make the original DC-to-store plan no longer well balanced.

---

## 3. Baseline Demand Plan: DC to Stores

The **baseline demand plan** is the initial replenishment decision from the DC to stores before any disruption or realized demand deviation occurs.

### 3.1 Purpose of the baseline plan

The purpose of this stage is to create a feasible and cost-efficient replenishment schedule that:

- satisfies forecasted store demand,
- respects inventory balance constraints,
- considers transportation decisions from the DC to stores,
- manages holding and shortage cost in the initial planning stage.

### 3.2 Interpretation

This baseline plan can be viewed as the **planned distribution policy** under normal conditions. It answers questions such as:

- How much of each SKU should the DC send to each store?
- In which period should replenishment happen?
- How much inventory should remain at the store after replenishment and demand fulfillment?

### 3.3 Role in the thesis

The baseline stage is important because it creates the reference solution from which later imbalance is measured. Without a baseline plan, there is no structured way to determine whether a store has become short or surplus after demand changes.

Thus, the thesis assumes that the baseline DC-to-store plan is generated first, using planned demand data.

---

## 4. Demand Fluctuation Through Shocking Factor

After the baseline plan is created, actual store demand may no longer match the planned demand. To model this, the thesis introduces a **shocking factor**.

### 4.1 Meaning of the shocking factor

The shocking factor represents the deviation between forecast demand and realized demand. It can be interpreted as a multiplicative or scenario-based disturbance applied to the original demand values.

For example:

- if forecast demand is 100 units and the shocking factor is 1.20, realized demand becomes 120 units,
- if forecast demand is 100 units and the shocking factor is 0.80, realized demand becomes 80 units.

This mechanism allows the thesis to simulate demand volatility in a controlled way.

### 4.2 Why it matters

Once the shocking factor changes demand, the original baseline replenishment may no longer be optimal or even sufficient:

- A store with higher-than-expected realized demand may face shortage.
- A store with lower-than-expected realized demand may carry excess stock.
- The DC shipment plan has already been decided in the baseline stage, so the system needs a second-stage response.

This is exactly the reason why lateral transshipment is introduced.

---

## 5. Why Lateral Transshipment Is Needed

Lateral transshipment is needed because the baseline plan is based on forecast demand, while actual realized demand may differ after the shocking factor is applied.
In this thesis, lateral transshipment is not treated merely as an emergency inventory movement, but as a cost-optimizing corrective mechanism after demand disruption. The baseline replenishment plan from the DC to stores is first established based on forecast demand. However, once actual demand deviates from forecast through the shocking factor, inventory imbalances emerge across stores. In such cases, lateral transshipment plays a crucial role in redistributing inventory from surplus stores to shortage stores, thereby mitigating stockout risk, reducing unnecessary emergency replenishment from the DC, utilizing available inventory more efficiently, and improving the overall total cost of the system.

### 5.1 Core logic

The logic is:

- The DC sends products to stores according to the baseline plan.
- Real demand is then realized with demand shocks.
- Some stores now have **surplus inventory**, while others have **shortage inventory**.
- Instead of relying only on another DC dispatch, the system allows **store-to-store transfers**.

### 5.2 Benefit of LT

Lateral transshipment may improve system performance by:

- reducing stockout and shortage cost,
- utilizing excess inventory already available in the network,
- avoiding unnecessary waste or overstock,
- improving service level,
- potentially reducing response time when compared with waiting for a new DC shipment.

### 5.3 Role in the thesis framework

In this thesis, LT is treated as a **second-stage balancing mechanism** activated only when the realized demand pattern creates meaningful imbalance across stores.

This makes the framework more realistic than solving only a static baseline replenishment problem.

---

## 6. Overall Thesis Problem Statement

The thesis addresses a **multi-period, multi-store inventory routing and inventory rebalancing problem** under demand fluctuation.

The overall decision setting is:

- The DC creates a baseline replenishment plan for stores.
- Actual demand deviates from forecast due to shocking factors.
- Inventory imbalance appears across stores.
- The system evaluates whether lateral transshipment between stores should be performed.
- The objective is to minimize total system cost while maintaining feasible inventory and transportation decisions.

In short, the thesis studies how to integrate:

1. **baseline DC-to-store replenishment**, and
2. **post-disruption store-to-store lateral transshipment**

within one optimization-oriented framework.

---

## 7. General Optimization Perspective

From a modeling perspective, the thesis combines two decision layers:

### Stage 1: Baseline IRP planning

This stage determines the initial replenishment from the DC to stores over the planning horizon.

Typical cost components include:

- DC-to-store transportation cost,
- inventory holding cost,
- shortage or unmet-demand penalty,
- possible vehicle or routing cost if routing is explicitly modeled.

### Stage 2: Rebalancing through lateral transshipment

After demand changes, this stage determines whether inventory should be reallocated among stores.

Typical cost and decision considerations include:

- store-to-store transportation cost,
- quantity transferred between donor and receiver stores,
- shortage reduction effect,
- remaining surplus or shortage after transfer,
- feasibility of LT activation under defined rules or thresholds.

---

## 8. Intended Methodological Direction

The broader methodological direction of the thesis is not only to model the operational problem, but also to solve it efficiently for larger instances.

The intended framework includes:

- a **baseline optimization model** for DC-to-store replenishment,
- a **column generation structure** to represent candidate lateral transshipment patterns,
- possible **branch-and-price or restricted master problem logic** for scalable solution search,
- and a **GNN-assisted selection/ranking mechanism** to improve the efficiency of choosing promising columns.

Within this structure:

- the baseline demand plan provides the initial state,
- the shocking factor creates post-plan demand deviation,
- and lateral transshipment columns represent recovery patterns for redistributing stock across stores.

## 9. Expected Thesis Outputs

The expected outputs of the thesis are not limited to a final mathematical model. The thesis is expected to deliver several layers of results.

### 9.1 Conceptual output

A clear problem formulation that explains:

- the retail distribution context,
- the baseline DC-to-store replenishment logic,
- the impact of demand fluctuation,
- and the role of lateral transshipment as a corrective balancing mechanism.

### 9.2 Mathematical/modeling output

A model or integrated framework that defines:

- indices, sets, and parameters,
- decision variables,
- objective function,
- inventory balance constraints,
- replenishment and transshipment logic,
- and feasibility rules for multi-period operation.

### 9.3 Algorithmic output

A solution framework capable of generating and selecting promising LT patterns, especially for larger instances where exhaustive enumeration is inefficient.

This may include:

- baseline solver output,
- column generation iterations,
- reduced cost evaluation,
- and possibly GNN-guided column ranking.

### 9.4 Computational output

The thesis should produce numerical experiments showing:

- baseline replenishment results,
- demand shock scenarios,
- transshipment decisions after shock realization,
- total cost comparison with and without LT,
- service level or shortage improvements,
- and computational performance of the proposed method.

### 9.5 Managerial output

The thesis should also provide insight into when lateral transshipment is useful, such as:

- under what level of demand volatility LT becomes valuable,
- which stores tend to become donors or receivers,
- whether LT reduces total shortage significantly,
- and whether the proposed framework improves responsiveness compared with DC-only replenishment.

---

## 10. Desired Final Outcome of the Thesis Work

The desired end result of the thesis is a practical and academically sound framework that demonstrates the following idea:

> A retail network should not rely only on a static DC-to-store replenishment plan. Because realized demand can deviate from forecast through demand shocks, the system should include a second-stage inventory rebalancing mechanism through lateral transshipment.

More specifically, the thesis aims to show that:

- the **baseline replenishment plan** is necessary as the first planning layer,
- the **shocking factor** realistically creates post-plan imbalance,
- **lateral transshipment** is justified as a recovery mechanism,
- and an advanced optimization / learning-assisted framework can solve this integrated problem more effectively.

---

## 11. Summary in One Paragraph

This thesis studies a retail inventory routing problem in which a central DC first creates a baseline replenishment plan for stores based on expected demand. After that, realized demand changes due to a shocking factor, causing some stores to face shortages and others to hold surplus stock. To correct this imbalance, the system allows lateral transshipment between stores. The thesis therefore develops an integrated framework that links baseline DC-to-store planning with post-shock store-to-store rebalancing, with the goal of reducing total system cost, improving service performance, and supporting scalable solution methods such as column generation and GNN-assisted column selection.
